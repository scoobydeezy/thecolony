history: |-
  The Witnesses began as a protest of conscience within the earliest structures of the Order. The ritual may have been necessary, but some could not accept that necessity required silence. If a person willingly carried the burden for the sake of the Colony, then their passing deserved more than administration. It deserved reverence. The first Witnesses did not seek to end the ritual. They sought to ensure that every sacrifice was seen, honored, and mourned.

  What began as quiet acts of remembrance gradually became a solemn responsibility. The Witnesses gathered letters, tools, jewelry, unfinished artwork, and other belongings left behind by the Forgotten, preserving them within the Hall of Remembrance and recording them in the Ledger. Though names inevitably faded from the record, the objects themselves endured. Over time, the Witnesses came to recognize that some possessions carried more than memory. Items burdened with profound emotional weight could leave subtle fractures in the world around them, unsettling those who encountered them. Preserving these relics became not only an act of remembrance, but an act of stewardship, keeping dangerous wounds from quietly spreading through the Colony.

  As their duties expanded, the Witnesses established the House of Abiding, where those chosen for the ritual spend their final days in peace while their belongings are gathered for preservation before memory fades. Their ceremonies evolved as well. The Empty Lectern became the center of memorial services where the stories of the Forgotten could be spoken aloud, even as the names themselves disappeared from every page and every mind. In this way, the Witnesses sought to preserve dignity where identity could not survive.

  Today, the Witnesses remain the Colony's conscience, preserving the traces of lives that would otherwise disappear entirely. Their halls hold generations of forgotten belongings, each catalogued with painstaking care, while the Ledger continues to grow despite its empty spaces. They believe no sacrifice should vanish without remembrance, even if they have come to accept that remembrance itself cannot change the burden they faithfully record.
