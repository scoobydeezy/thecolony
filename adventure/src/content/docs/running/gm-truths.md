## The First Law

> **Memory has weight. It cannot be destroyed. It can only be carried.**

This is not philosophy or religion. It is a property of reality. Most of the time the weight is diffuse, spread across countless lives, and its effects are small: a familiarity no one can explain, a place that feels haunted without reason, a dream that lingers too long. People explain them away as déjà vu, grief, coincidence.

The Veiled, the Reading, Fractured Objects, the eclipse and the Forgotten all follow from this one rule.

## The Book of Names

The :ref[Book of Names]{#source.book-of-names} did not begin as an artifact. It began as an ordinary memorial register: someone in the early Colony (one person, or many) recording the names of the dead, the disappeared and the unclaimed, so that they would not vanish without acknowledgement. No one knows who began it.

It has no title, sigil or ornament; its pages hold no dates or histories, only names. The earliest pages are crowded, names squeezed into margins and between lines. Toward the middle, the pattern changes: each page bears a single name.

The Book did not begin with power. It became heavy.

## Aletheia, the First Keeper

**Aletheia** kept the memorial register before the office of Keeper, the Reading or the Order of Theia existed. She read every name not as a clerk reviewing entries but as evidence of a whole life, and accepted that those lives remained connected to hers.

At the final page she did something no keeper of the register had done before: **she wrote her own name.** No ritual existed. No doctrine promised anything.

Memory moved toward her. The sun went dark behind a shadow, with no moon in the sky. When the light returned, Aletheia was gone, and the Colony had forgotten she ever existed. It remembered only the shape of what had happened:

- Someone had read every name.
- Someone had written their own.
- An incredible weight of memory pressed in.
- The sky had darkened.
- The pressure subsided.
- The light returned.

The register became the surviving anchor of her relationship with the dead, and its weight deepened. In time the weight spilled out again, and the Colony, in desperation, repeated the sequence. The next bearer encountered not only the names but Aletheia herself. Every new name carries the burden forward, and adds another life to what must someday be carried.

Procedure became tradition. Tradition became doctrine. Doctrine became the Reading. The **Order of Theia** unknowingly preserves a fragment of her name.

## The Calling and the Reading

The Reading has no calendar. It comes when accumulated pressure grows too great for the Colony to carry diffusely: on average about once a year, but never reliably; six months, eighteen, sometimes two or three years. As the pressure rises, memory moves toward a potential bearer. The Colony calls this **the Calling**, and the Called person becomes the next **Keeper**.

The signs begin subtly: vivid dreams of unfamiliar lives, recognising places never visited, emotions without remembered causes, names spoken without knowing their meaning. The Colony can recognise them. It cannot create them, reliably predict them, or choose who experiences them.

A Calling is a celebrated honour. When the Reading begins, the Keeper reads every name in the Book, and for one impossible span encounters the lives gathered there: the dead first recorded, the people and relationships attached to them, and every Keeper since Aletheia.

- Reading the names creates **Recognition**.
- Experiencing the lives creates **Relationship and Meaning**.
- Writing their own name expresses **Acceptance**.

As the Keeper reads, the moonless eclipse begins and deepens; at the final page it reaches Totality. The Keeper writes their name, memory gathers into them, and the pressure settles. The shadow moves away. The Keeper is gone, and the Colony forgets they ever existed. Their name stays in the Book; in the living world they become an absence. Nothing has been destroyed. The burden has been redistributed.

:::gm-note
**The Keeper's willingness is real.** The Reading is not a sham, and no one controls the Keeper. A Keeper who reaches enough of the Book may sincerely choose to continue, because the lives they encounter become real. They do not think *I must save the Colony*. They think: **I cannot allow those who carried me to be forgotten.** Play Vara's choice, whatever it is, as hers.
:::

## The Answer that Forgot Its Question

The Book really is powerful, the Ritual really relieves pressure, the eclipse really responds, and the Colony's procedures really protect lives. But the register was created so that responsibility could be **shared**: the dead belonged to the whole community's memory.

The modern Ritual reverses that. The Colony concentrates its inherited responsibility in one willing person, and then spares everyone else not only the burden but even the grief of remembering who carried it. For the Keeper, the Reading remains an act of sincere remembrance. For the Colony, it has become an institution of forgetting.

No one designed this inversion. It emerged through generations of repetition: the Colony preserved the sequence of Aletheia's actions and lost the question that gave them meaning.

> **The injustice is institutional, not illusory.**

## The Eclipse

The eclipse is the visible sign of mnemonic transfer. Under the Reading it advances as the Keeper reads, and normally moves away once the Book accepts the Keeper's name.

It can also happen outside the ritual, whenever recognition, relationship and acceptance become coherent enough for memory to start moving toward another destination. This is why, if the Reading succeeds, the inheritance can later reopen without a new Called Keeper.

## The Forgotten and the Veiled

The **Forgotten** are the real people carried in the inheritance. The **Veiled** are not those people: they are false reconstructions, made when memory tries to bring a forgotten life back without enough Identity to rebuild it correctly. A Veiled can be emotionally truthful and historically false. Combat can disperse a manifestation; it cannot erase the weight beneath it. See The Veiled: A Practical Primer.

## Vara and Marek

:::secret
**Vara Cairn**, the newly Called Keeper, is fifteen. Her own father, **Marek Cairn**, was the previous Keeper, eighteen months ago. Vara does not remember that she ever had a father; the Colony believes her an orphan. The cartographer's compass she believes she has always owned was his.
:::

## The Observer

:::secret
The **Observer** is not the campaign's villain. He is its central question made into a person: a man who has spent decades studying how institutions change, how every answer to a real problem hardens into tradition, doctrine and certainty, until it creates the problems it once solved.

> **Can people build something better without eventually becoming the thing they fought?**

The Colony is not his experiment; it is his evidence. **The Party is his experiment.** He does not manipulate outcomes; he creates conditions, asks questions and watches what emerges. He hopes, without quite believing it, that Darkwing will prove his conclusions incomplete.
:::

## The Light Remains

Across the Colony, farewells end with a phrase: **"The light remains."** It is said at meals, at funerals, before journeys, when leaving an empty chair at the table, and by the Keeper who enters the Ritual Chamber. No one remembers where it came from. Children hear a blessing; adults hear that hope endures, as the sun endures behind an eclipse. So too with memory: the Forgotten are never erased.

:::gm-note
**Never define the phrase.** The campaign should not reveal one secret, authoritative meaning of "the light remains". The final answer the Party and the Colony reach should give the phrase a new meaning, chosen by the living Colony.
:::
