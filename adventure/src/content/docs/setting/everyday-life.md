The Colony has made room for people it cannot remember. Its customs are useful, generous, and familiar to the people who practice them. Let the Party enjoy that hospitality before it recognizes the shape of the losses beneath it.

## Traditions and Empty Spaces

| Custom | Ordinary practice | What the GM can let the players notice |
|---|---|---|
| **The Open Place** | A household leaves one chair unoccupied. A guest may use it; a household member does not routinely do so. Taverns and civic gatherings preserve versions of the custom. | Hospitality has made an absence look intentional: “A full table has no room for guests.” |
| **The Second Portion** | A meal includes one extra portion, left by the open place or hearth, then given to someone who needs it. | A household can keep feeding one more person than it remembers having. Its present generosity is real. |
| **Nothing Is Ownerless** | An object without a known owner is “in passage.” It enters another home, workshop, storehouse, or market. Persistent questions about an ordinary object's origin are faintly rude. | Possessions circulate after their owners vanish from memory. “It came to the house” may be an honest account. |
| **The Unfinished Likeness** | Portraits leave space: a gap between workers, an empty chair, an off-center family. | A picture can remain compositionally complete when someone is missing. “A household is larger than those who could stand for the painting.” |
| **The Spare Hand** | Workshops teach overlapping duties; offices share authority; households teach children work beyond their usual role. | Resilience lets necessary work continue after its bearer is forgotten. “No work should fail because one pair of hands is absent.” |
| **The Resting Stroke** | Bells, songs, prayers, and recitations leave a deliberate final silence. Children learn not to fill it. | The missing beat belongs to the music. “The silence is part of the measure.” |

These customs need no fixed hidden origin. Some may have begun as direct responses to a forgotten person; others may have changed through generations of repetition. Their shared payoff is the players' recognition of how the Colony lives with absence.

## Objects in Circulation

A repaired tool, worn chair, or book on an unfamiliar shelf may outlast any conscious memory of its owner. Most such objects remain ordinary possessions. Some retain enough emotional weight to become Fractured Objects; the :ref[Witnesses]{#faction.witnesses} try to identify and preserve them before redistribution.

Show wear and use before explaining significance. A repair suggests care, a repeated alteration suggests habit, and an object preserved beyond its usefulness suggests a relationship. These details give players evidence they can return to after their understanding changes.

## Public Ceremony and Private Care

A Calling is celebrated. The Keeper receives honor and care, and the procession makes that recognition public. The :ref[House of Abiding]{#location.house-of-abiding} receives the Called Keeper; the Witnesses learn the significance of their belongings while someone can still explain them. The :ref[Memorial Building]{#location.memorial-building} and :ref[Empty Lectern]{#asset.empty-lectern} give remembrance a public form.

Play each ceremony through the people performing it. Uldren's care, a neighbor's pride, and a guard's concern can all be sincere. The opening procession and Vara's reception belong to :ref[The Calling]{#event.the-calling} and :ref[The House of Abiding]{#event.the-house-of-abiding}; use those scenes for their particular sequence and choices.

## The Light Remains

“The light remains” closes farewells, meals, funerals, and departures. It can accompany an empty chair or the Keeper's entrance into the Ritual Chamber. Children hear a blessing; adults may hear reassurance that hope persists through darkness.

No one remembers its origin. Do not reveal one authoritative translation at the finale. Let changed relationships and the Colony's chosen future give the familiar phrase a new meaning.

## Bringing a Street to Life

For an ordinary scene, choose a practical task, a person doing it, and one visible custom. A baker sets aside the second portion while dealing with a late delivery; an apprentice takes over a repair using the spare hand's training. These are staging suggestions, not additional fixed institutions or required events.

Repeat a few familiar details across the campaign. After a loss, return to the table, workshop, or portrait and let the players see what has changed. After a hopeful outcome, show who now has a place there.
