history: |-
  The Dawn Assembly began among Witnesses who could no longer bear the distance between remembrance and change. They had spent years honoring the Forgotten, preserving their belongings, and comforting the grieving, only to watch the ritual continue year after year. Over time, some came to a painful conclusion: the Colony had learned how to mourn without transforming. They believed remembrance was not enough. The burden belonged to everyone, and the future could not simply become another repetition of the past.

  Their earliest gatherings took place before sunrise in workshops, courtyards, and unfinished public spaces beyond the reach of the Order. Dawn became both a meeting time and a symbol of the future they hoped to build. What began as quiet conversations soon grew into organized petitions, public proposals, and open criticism of the Colony's oldest institutions. They believed the ritual's burden should be shared rather than concealed, and that a better society could be built through collective responsibility rather than inherited tradition.

  Over time, the movement became too popular to ignore. The Assembly Hall gave the Dawn a legitimate place within civic life, while the Dawn Charter transformed scattered demands into a living record of reforms and aspirations. Through the Civic Improvement Fund and the Public Works Yard, the Assembly proved its ideals through roads, bridges, schools, gardens, and public works that improved daily life throughout the Colony. They did not merely argue for a better future. They began building one.

  Today, the Dawn Assembly remains the Colony's most credible political alternative to the Order, advocating gradual reform, public accountability, and shared responsibility for the burden. Yet each success has carried an unexpected cost. As the Assembly became part of civic life, it also became part of the system it once opposed. The Colony grew more compassionate, more prosperous, and more hopeful—but the ritual endured all the same.
